## 1. Purpose and constraints

### 1.1 Purpose

**Where does the training data come from, and how do we know we are allowed to use it?** Not "will the model be good" — that belongs to `benchmark-and-evaluation-plan.md`. This document is about **provenance, permission, quality, and reproducibility**: the four properties that make a corpus usable in a project whose premise is independence.

### 1.2 The binding legal constraint

**[KNOWN FACT]** Anthropic Commercial Terms §D.4 "Use Restrictions" prohibit accessing the Services "to build a competing product or service", including "to train competing AI models", except as expressly approved by Anthropic. Source: `https://www.anthropic.com/legal/commercial-terms`, observed 2026-10-21 by `licensing-and-provenance-review.md` §2.

**[OBSERVED]** `licensing-and-provenance-review.md` §8.2 adopts the operative rule: *"A training corpus for our own model must have no path to the reference product."*

**[INFERRED]** The rule is not a precaution; it is what makes the project coherent. Remove the path and the question of §D.4 does not arise. The corpus becomes auditable by construction, not by assertion.

**EXCLUDED, unconditionally, from every corpus, every stage, every mixture:**

| Excluded input | Why | Cross-reference |
| :- | :- | :- |
| The reference product's completions, tool-call sequences, transcripts | §D.4 — distillation from the Services is training a competing model | `licensing-and-provenance-review.md` §8.2 |
| The prompt research collection, in whole or in paraphrase | The captured vendor text is output of the Services; a CC0 root grant does not cover captured content | §3.2, §3.3 |
| The six Anthropic-licensed skill bundles inside it | Carry Anthropic's own restrictive licence forbidding extraction and derivative works | §4 |
| Synthetic data generated by the reference product | Output of the Services | §8.2 |
| Anything scraped from a coding agent's live traffic | Same reason | §8.2 |
| Anthropic model weights or derivatives | Market question; §D.4's phrase is "competing AI models" | §8.2 |

**[OBSERVED]** `prompt-pattern-analysis.md` §5 records, per principle category, the independent implementation and the test that proves it. **Reading a reference product's behaviour is permitted; training on its output is not.** This document uses only the first.

### 1.3 What the constraint shapes

**[INFERRED]** Four concrete design decisions follow, and each is visible later in this document:

1. **No teacher.** No corpus row is a distillation target; we never call a model to generate training text. Every SFT row in §6 comes from a permissively-licensed published dataset whose licence we verified, or from our own hand-written data.
2. **Every corpus row has a named upstream licensor** — not "the dataset's licence" but the licence of whoever wrote the code, because that is the licence that travels into our weights. Hence §2's two licence columns and §9's per-item attribution field.
3. **Contamination control is a legal control, not only a measurement control.** `benchmark-and-evaluation-plan.md` §5.3 requires the training-data filter to cover the *entire* suite. §3 implements that as a pipeline stage: a corpus with no demonstrable connection to the Services is the strongest available evidence that §D.4 was respected.
4. **No API calls are made to build this corpus.** Every figure in §10 is an egress or CPU figure. A corpus we had to buy from the reference service would have a path to the reference service.

### 1.4 Operating rules for this workstream

**[OBSERVED]** Restatements of rules established elsewhere, applied to data work. Binding on the pipeline and its operators.

| # | Rule | Source |
| :- | :- | :- |

**In scope:** corpora for pretraining, continued pretraining, SFT, and (if it happens) preference optimisation of our own model; the filters, dedup, mixture, packing, provenance and cost of that data.

**Out of scope:** the reference product's data; the benchmark suite's data; harness runtime behaviour; inference-time data; anything requiring a paid API call.

## 2. Corpus register

### 2.1 Two licence columns, and why

**[OBSERVED]** The register has two licence columns because they are different things:

- **Packaging licence** — what the *publisher* placed on the distributed artefact. Verified from Hub `cardData.license` or the card's `license:` YAML line, 2026-10-21.
- **Per-item licence** — the licence of the *underlying material*, the one that travels into the weights: for code, the individual repository's licence; for web text, the page publisher's licence, which a packaging licence cannot adjudicate.

**[INFERRED]** Where the packaging licence is permissive but the per-item licence is "varies", the permissive label is a permission for the *packaging*, not the material. Hence the register has both a "commercial training permitted?" and a "D.4-safe?" column.

### 2.2 Verdict vocabulary

| Verdict | Meaning |
| :- | :- |
| **USE** | Ingestible under the standard pipeline, subject to a normal §9 ledger entry. |
| **USE-WITH-CONDITIONS** | Ingestible **only if** the listed conditions are met and recorded. If a condition cannot be met, this collapses to EXCLUDE. |
| **EXCLUDE** | Not ingested. The licence forbids our use; the material is barred by §D.4; or it is an evaluation asset whose training use would destroy the evaluation. |
| **DEFER** | Decision postponed until a named precondition is satisfied — usually an unverified licence check, or a roadmap stage not yet reached. |

**[OBSERVED]** "D.4-safe?" answers one question: **does this corpus have any path to the reference product?** Third-party code, published by third parties under third-party licences, with no generation step involving the reference product, is D.4-safe by construction. Model-generated instruction data is D.4-safe only if the generating model is identifiable and is not the reference product.

### 2.2a The contingency if no licence-clean corpus exists at scale

**[INFERRED — the gap this section closes]** `llm-development-roadmap.md` §17.3 rule 6 makes the corpus requirement absolute: *"The corpus must have no path to the reference product's outputs. **This is absolute** and applies to Stages D, F, G and H."* The strictness is correct and this document endorses it without qualification. **What was missing was a plan for the failure case.** The roadmap's unknown `U6` records that *whether a licence-clean corpus of sufficient size and quality exists* is **[UNKNOWN]** — and `U6` is not answerable by inspection, only by doing the §9 gate per candidate corpus. **A plan that depends on an unknown resolving favourably, with no alternative, is a plan that has not yet failed.**

**[INFERRED]** At the token scale the ladder needs (23–30 B tokens at R2), a corpus with genuinely uniform permissive terms and no per-file attribution burden is a narrow thing to find. The permissive corpora that exist at that scale — The Stack, RefinedWeb, FineWeb and their derivatives — carry per-item licence metadata, and the attribution-discharge mechanism `U6` names does not currently exist. **This section states the contingency that runs if that search comes back negative. It is not a licence weakening: every branch below still satisfies rule 6, because rule 6 is about provenance of the *content*, not about how big the corpus is.**

**The contingency, in order of preference.** Each branch is strictly less capable than a clean large corpus and strictly more expensive, which is the correct direction for a contingency.

| Branch | What it is | What it costs | What it preserves |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **1 — Attribution-discharge** | Use a per-item-licence corpus (The Stack v2 family), but build the mechanism its terms require: per-item provenance ledger, SWHID publication for Stack-derived data, per-licence attribution shards, and a **model release under a suitable open licence** with the SWH statement's conditions met | Engineering cost of the ledger and the shard machinery; the ODC-By/SA questions in §2.5 (OQ-D1) need an answer before release, not before training | **All capability.** The corpus is large and high quality; the cost is legal machinery, not data quality |
| **2 — Single-licence regeneration** | Restrict the corpus to genuinely single-licence artefacts (Regime 1 in §2.4) — `odc-by` web corpora, `cdla-permissive-2.0` instruction sets, MIT/Apache-2.0-licensed code selected per-file with a licence classifier | Smaller corpus; more filtering; a real chance of not reaching 23–30 B tokens of *code* | The cleanest possible provenance; the fewest obligations |
| **3 — Self-generated data** | Generate the corpus ourselves: our own written code, our own hand-written SFT rows, and — where a teacher is needed — **a permissively-licensed open model that is not the reference product** (the base-model candidates in `compute-feasibility.md` §13.2 are Apache-2.0, so their outputs inherit a licence we can discharge) | The most expensive branch: we pay for a teacher model, and our data inherits that model's quality ceiling | Complete D.4-safety with no third-party attribution burden at all. **This branch also produces the only corpus that is unambiguously ours** |
| **4 — Scale down, do not scale across** | If no branch yields enough tokens, **reduce the rung**: train R1 (7.2 B tokens) rather than R2 (30 B), and accept a smaller model rather than a licence-encumbered one | A weaker model, and a smaller claim | The licence position, at the cost of capability. **This is the honest floor** |

**[INFERRED]** **Branch 3 deserves particular attention, because it inverts the project's usual logic.** The rest of the plan treats "use a permissively-licensed open model" as a *compromise* — something you do while waiting to train your own. Here, in the failure case, an open model with an Apache-2.0 licence is the *safest* teacher available, because its outputs carry a licence we can name, discharge, and attribute, whereas the alternative is an aggregate of thousands of unknown per-item licences. **A teacher's licence travels into the data, and an Apache-2.0 teacher produces data with a known, dischargeable licence — which is strictly better than "thousands of per-item licences, one of which may be incompatible."** This is also the branch that keeps the project's independence claim intact: the teacher is a *published open model*, not the reference product, so rule 6 is satisfied by construction.

**[INFERRED] The contingency is bound to Stage A, not Stage D.** The corpus search — the §9 five-item gate, applied per candidate — must begin at **Stage A (tokenizer)**, in parallel with tokenizer design, because the corpus *shape* determines the tokenizer's vocabulary design (per §7.1, code-heavy corpora need different vocabulary allocation than prose-heavy ones), and because a Stage-A-delayed corpus search would not surface a `U6` negative until Stage D, by which point Stages B and C have consumed their budget against a premise that has since failed. **The search is cheap — it is a licence review, not a training run — and it is the cheapest possible early warning.**

**[OBSERVED] What would actually settle `U6`, in order:** (a) run the §9 five-item gate on the three or four largest single-licence candidates in §2.3's Regime-1 block and report the token counts each actually yields after filtering; (b) run the same gate on one large Regime-2 candidate with a per-item licence classifier, and report the fraction of items that are unambiguously permissive; (c) measure the token yield of branch 3 at a realistic filter rate. **[UNKNOWN]** — the project has not yet run (a), (b), or (c), and no number in this document should be read as the result of any of them.

### 2.4 The two regimes, stated separately

**[INFERRED]** The register reduces to two regimes, and nearly every hard decision follows from which regime a corpus is in.

**Regime 1 — single-licence packaging.** One licence on the whole artefact; the content is public-domain-ish or compatibly licensed. Examples: FineWeb/FineWeb-Edu/SmolLM-Corpus/Dolma/Tulu 3 (`odc-by`), C3/C4/C8/C9/C13/C18, orca-agentinstruct (`cdla-permissive-2.0`). **Consequence:** one ledger row per corpus; attribution + share-alike flags; the §9.3 shard rule where SA exists.

**Regime 2 — per-item licence inheritance.** An aggregate: the packaging licence describes the packaging; the content carries thousands of individual licences. Examples: The Stack v2, GitHub Code, CodeSearchNet, SWE-bench. **Consequence:** the ledger must be per-*item*; the licence filter excludes per-item licences, not the dataset's label; attribution survives into the model.

## 3. Contamination control

### 3.1 Two different problems, deliberately not conflated

**[INFERRED]** "Contamination" has two distinct meanings here, and merging them would produce a plan that is either too expensive or too weak.

| Problem | Definition | Where measured | Owner |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **C-A. Evaluation contamination** | Training data contains content that makes an evaluation task easier than it should be | `benchmark-and-evaluation-plan.md` §4.6 | Benchmark plan; this document supplies the filter |
| **C-B. D.4 contamination** | Training data contains any path to the reference product | §1.2 | This document, absolutely |

**[INFERRED]** C-A is a measurement-quality problem: it degrades confidence in results. C-B is a legal problem: it invalidates the project. **We treat C-B as a hard filter and C-A as a best-effort filter with published residual risk.**

### 3.2 The exclusion that is absolute

**[KNOWN FACT]** `licensing-and-provenance-review.md` §8.2 excludes the prompt research collection from training use. **[OBSERVED]** The collection is CC0-at-root, but **6 captured Anthropic skill bundles carry Anthropic's own restrictive licence and are EXCLUDED**.

**[INFERRED]** The template for every similar decision: **a licence on the container does not travel backwards to the content inside it.** A CC0 grant cannot license Anthropic's own text. The content is output of the Services; ingesting it would be distillation; and R1/R2 forbid copying prompt text and treating any captured prompt as data. **Verdict: EXCLUDE, permanently.**

### 3.3 The 5.x / 4.x corroboration finding

**[OBSERVED]** From the project's own evidence base (`licensing-and-provenance-review.md` and the prompt-analysis working notes):

- **5.x captures**: 86–92% of Anthropic's own published prompt text appears **verbatim**.
- **4.x captures**: only 14–29% appears.

**[INFERRED]** The difference is evidence about *how the captures were made*, with a direct consequence: **a capture's authority depends on its verification rate, and a partially-verified capture is not a weaker corpus — it is evidence of an unknown, and unknowns do not become training data by being plausible.** This is why it is excluded whole: "verified output of the Services" cannot be separated from "unverified content of unknown origin" by inspection.

**[UNKNOWN]** The per-capture verification percentages for any individual capture are not recorded in a form this document can cite precisely. **What would settle it:** the capture ledger in the prompt-analysis working notes, which records the verification rate per capture; we would cite per-capture figures there, not here.

### 3.4 What we do *not* claim

**[OBSERVED]** The project's standing rules, restated because this is where a reader might be tempted to over-claim:

- Inspecting a coding agent **does not** reveal private model weights or training methods.
- The prompt research collection **does not** prove a behaviour exists in the running product. It proves text was published. Behaviour claims require the benchmark plan's controlled measurement, not the collection.
- The 5.x/4.x corroboration gap **does not** license any usage. It is context for why the collection is excluded on independent grounds.

### 3.5 C-A: the five filters

**[INFERRED]** Applied in pipeline order. Each is cheap before the one after it.

| # | Filter | Method | Removes | Cost | Residual risk |
| :- | :- | :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **1** | **Exact hash** | SHA-256 over normalised content; drop files whose digest is in the suite set | Byte-identical copies of evaluation files | ~2 CPU-hr/100 GB (§4) | Files edited cosmetically survive |
| **2** | **N-gram overlap** | 13-gram MinHash + LSH; drop documents with Jaccard ≥ 0.8 vs any suite document | Near-copies, reformattings, boilerplate | ~6 CPU-hr/100 GB | Long documents shift content; threshold is a compromise |
| **3** | **Git history** | Match corpus commits to suite repos by SWHID / commit-graph similarity | Repos whose *history* holds the solution, even if the current file differs | ~4 CPU-hr/100 GB | Needs git metadata the corpus may lack |
| **4** | **Repository co-occurrence** | Drop any corpus item belonging to a repository that appears in the suite | Whole repositories, including unrelated files | ~1 CPU-hr/100 GB | **Over-removes.** A suite repo costs us its entire non-suite content |
| **5** | **Exact-patch near-dup** | Normalised-diff matching of candidate patches vs suite gold patches | Solutions reached by a different route | ~2 CPU-hr/100 GB | Misses semantically equivalent, syntactically different solutions |

**[INFERRED]** Filter 4 hurts and we keep it, because a benchmark whose repositories were partly in training is not a benchmark — it is a measurement of recall. Over-removal is bounded and quantified in §5; under-removal is unbounded and unmeasurable.

**[OBSERVED]** `benchmark-and-evaluation-plan.md` §4.6 already lists seven contamination controls and their honest limits; we do not duplicate them. This table is the *implementation-side* view. Filter 5 is our addition, and the only one needing a corpus to carry diffs rather than final files.

### 3.6 The four held-out levels and five partitions

**[OBSERVED]** `benchmark-and-evaluation-plan.md` §5.1 defines four held-out levels: **L-task** (task instances), **L-repo** (whole repositories), **L-file** (files in a repository), **L-checkpoint** (model states). §5.2 defines **five** partitions: **DEV**, **TEST-A**, **LIVE** (the rotating release-gate partition), **TEST-B**, and the **RESERVE pool**. §5.3 requires the training-data filter to cover the *entire* suite.

> **[OBSERVED] Correction (2026-10-22).** This subsection previously said "four partitions: DEV, TEST-A, TEST-B, RESERVE". The partition structure changed when the evaluation plan's three inconsistent statements about TEST-B were reconciled (its §7.2 EVAL-03 correction). **The LIVE partition is new, and it has a direct consequence for this document: a LIVE slice is quarantined from tuning *while it is in rotation*, and the RESERVE pool now holds two generations of LIVE tasks rather than being opened only for emergencies.** A model trained on anything derived from a LIVE slice — including the *aggregated* content of gate transcripts — has contaminated that slice, and the slice must be retired rather than reused.

**[INFERRED]** The mapping from filter coverage to partition discipline:

| Level | What the corpus filter must guarantee | What it cannot guarantee | Partition use |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **L-task** | Filters 1, 2, 5 — no instance text or near-identical solution in the corpus | That a *different* problem does not appear | DEV + TEST-A/LIVE/TEST-B |
| **L-repo** | Filters 3, 4 — no suite repository has any content in the corpus | That a *fork* of the suite repository does not | DEV + TEST-A/LIVE/TEST-B |
| **L-file** | Filter 1 — no suite file, byte-exact | That a suite file's *function* is absent elsewhere | TEST-A |
| **L-checkpoint** | Nothing corpus-side; this is a training-dynamics property | — | RESERVE pool |

**[INFERRED]** L-checkpoint is the honest limit of corpus-side control. A mid-training checkpoint can memorise a task the corpus never contained, or generalise to it. Hence `benchmark-and-evaluation-plan.md` §5.4 requires a separately-sourced held-out set as an exit criterion: **the only defence against L-checkpoint leakage is a set the corpus provably never saw.**

### 3.7 The response ladder C1–C4

**[INFERRED]** The four-level response ladder for any contamination finding, used identically by the corpus filter and by evaluation:

| Level | Meaning | Corpus-side action | Eval-side action |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **C1** | Byte-identical match | Exclude the file; log the digest | Report as excluded; no penalty |
| **C2** | High n-gram overlap (≥ 0.8) | Exclude the file; log the pair | Report as excluded; no penalty |
| **C3** | Same repository, different file | Exclude the whole repository | Report as **repository-level caveat**; results flagged |
| **C4** | Semantic similarity to a gold patch, different surface | Exclude; log the similarity | Report as **semantic caveat**; results flagged and published |

**[INFERRED]** The ladder exists so that contamination is a *measurement* with a distribution, not a yes/no gate. A pipeline that reports only "clean" or "dirty" tells a reader nothing; one that reports a C1–C4 histogram per partition tells them how much to trust each number.

### 3.8 The suite fingerprint

**[INFERRED]** The suite is fingerprinted as a hash over the sorted list of its per-item digests, plus a second hash over its repository identifiers. The filter's input is the fingerprint, not a raw file list, so that:

1. The filter's input is versioned and auditable.
2. A suite change invalidates the filter run deterministically — a new suite version means a new filter run, and the old revision's fingerprint no longer matches, so it cannot be silently reused.
3. The fingerprint can be published without publishing the suite.

### 3.9 Residual risk, stated plainly

**[INFERRED]** No corpus-side filter is complete, and the plan says so. The known residual exposures, in descending order:

1. **Paraphrased solutions.** A model trained on 2021-era GitHub will have seen a Stack Overflow answer to a 2019 problem in different words. No n-gram filter catches this. This is the single largest residual and is not fixable at the corpus level.
2. **Benchmark-derived repositories not in the suite.** If a suite repository is a fork of, or vendored by, an unrelated popular repository, filter 4 misses it and filter 3 may not have the metadata to find it.
3. **Model-generated content inheriting a surface.** §2's synthetic corpora (C1, C2, C3, C9, C17) were generated by models that were themselves trained on Stack v2 and web corpora. Their contamination surface is inherited, not original.
4. **The evaluation suite's own private split.** `benchmark-and-evaluation-plan.md` §5.2's LIVE partition, TEST-B, and the RESERVE pool exist precisely to catch the case where we contaminate something we did not know was in the suite. The LIVE partition is the sharpest of the three, because it *rotates*: contamination there is not caught by a filter run against a static held-out set, but only by the slice-history audit (`benchmark-and-evaluation-plan.md` §5.3), which reports every slice, its task IDs, and the release cycle it served. This is why the filter must be re-run against the LIVE slice *in rotation* and not merely once against a frozen evaluation set, and why the RESERVE pool must hold two generations of slices — a slice that has been in rotation is a de-facto tuning set and must be retired rather than reused.

**[UNKNOWN]** The *magnitude* of residual exposure 1 is not measurable from our own evidence base without running a model. **What would settle it:** the near-dup analysis of §5's measurement gate (M1) plus the first checkpoint's behaviour on an L-task held-out set — a stage-N+1 experiment, not a planning-time fact.

## 4. Deduplication

### 4.1 Why dedup is a first-class concern here

**[INFERRED]** Two reasons, one technical, one legal.

**Technical.** Repeated material inflates the effective gradient steps per unique fact, so a model over-fits the repeated distribution and under-fits the tail. The classic case is a well-known repository vendored into thousands of projects.

**Legal.** In regime 2 (per-item licence inheritance), a duplicated file is duplicated **attribution obligation**. Dedup that removes copies of GPL code reduces the obligation count, and dedup that collapses the same file under different licences can *surface* the stricter licence. A licence-management action, not just a compute saving.

### 4.2 The three tiers

| Tier | Method | Threshold | Target | Cost |
| :- | :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **Exact** | SHA-256 over normalised content (strip trailing whitespace, normalise line endings, strip licence header before hashing **and keep the original**) | exact | Files, documents, whole-repo duplicates | ~2 CPU-hr/100 GB |
| **Fuzzy** | MinHash, 128 permutations, LSH banding (16 bands × 8 rows) | Jaccard ≥ **0.8** (provisional) | Reformatted copies, near-duplicate documents, licence-header churn | ~6 CPU-hr/100 GB |
| **Semantic** | Sentence-embedding cosine over fixed-size blocks | cosine ≥ **0.95** (provisional) | Semantic duplicates, boilerplate templates, generated-code patterns | ~3 CPU-hr/100 GB + ~40 GB weights |

**[OBSERVED]** The 0.8 and 0.95 thresholds are **provisional**. **What would settle them:** the M1 gate (§5.6) — a hand-adjudicated sample of 10,000 documents from each tier's boundary, reporting true precision/recall so the operating point is set from data, not convention.

### 4.3 Segment-level dedup, and vendored-code removal

**[INFERRED]** File-level dedup misses the most common duplication in real code: **a whole third-party library vendored inside a larger repository.** The file is not a duplicate of any other file; it is a duplicate of a *set* of files that also exist in their own right.

**Method.** For each repository, compute per-directory and per-file fingerprints; a directory whose file-set fingerprint matches a known standalone package's file-set is marked vendored and removed at directory granularity.

**[INFERRED]** Expected effect: vendored third-party code is commonly 5–20% of a large code corpus. **[ESTIMATE]** 1 TB of code at 3.7 bytes/token ≈ 2.7×10¹¹ tokens; at a 12% vendored fraction that is 3.2×10¹⁰ tokens removed → enough to cover R1's token requirement twice over. **The inputs are our own, unverified against any published measurement of this corpus family; what would settle it:** sample 1,000 repositories, compute the vendored fraction.

### 4.4 Token-budget effect

**[ESTIMATE]** Applied to our planned R1/R2 mixture, using the corpus composition in §6.2:

| Stage of the pipeline | Raw tokens | After exact dedup | After fuzzy dedup | After vendored removal | After semantic dedup |
| :- | :- | :- | :- | :- | :- |
| Code (Stack v2 subset + CommitPackFT + GitHub Code + docs) | 2.70×10¹¹ | 2.30×10¹¹ | 1.90×10¹¹ | 1.50×10¹¹ | 1.42×10¹¹ |
| Natural language (FineWeb-Edu + Dolma subsets) | 1.20×10¹² | 1.00×10¹² | 0.85×10¹² | — | 0.80×10¹² |
| Instruction (SFT corpora) | 8.0×10⁹ | 7.6×10⁹ | 7.0×10⑨ | — | 6.8×10⁹ |
| **Total** | **1.478×10¹²** | **1.238×10¹²** | **1.037×10¹²** | — | **9.67×10¹¹** |

Arithmetic: code 1.42×10¹¹ + NL 8.0×10¹¹ + instr 6.8×10⁹ = 9.67×10¹¹ tokens retained from 1.478×10¹² raw → **retention 65.4%**, i.e. **34.6% of raw tokens removed by deduplication**.

**[INFERRED]** The headline number is the consequence: **the dedup rate is the single largest determinant of whether a rung of the compute ladder is reachable with a given corpus.** At R1 (360M params, ~7.2B tokens Chinchilla-optimal per `compute-feasibility.md`), a 34.6% dedup rate means the raw corpus must be ~11B tokens to yield 7.2B; at R2 (1.5B, 30B optimal) ~46B; extended ~95B. These are the raw sizes our mixture must supply; §10 costs them.

**[UNKNOWN]** The dedup rates above (exact −14.8%, fuzzy −8.9%, vendored, semantic) are **[ESTIMATE]**s from typical published ranges for code and web corpora, applied to our mixture. **What would settle them:** running tiers 1–2 on a 1% stratified sample of the actual corpora (cost ~$20, ~1 CPU-day) and reporting the measured retention before committing to a mixture.

### 4.5 Cost

**[ESTIMATE]** For the 1.478×10¹² raw-token corpus (≈ 5.1×10¹² bytes ≈ 5.1 TB at 3.7 bytes/token):

| Tier | CPU-hours | Storage overhead | Note |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| Exact | 2 CPU-hr per 100 GB → 5.1 TB ÷ 100 GB × 2 = **102 CPU-hr** | ~0 (digest set: ~1 MB per million files) | Fully parallel, embarrassingly |
| Fuzzy | 6 CPU-hr per 100 GB → **306 CPU-hr** | LSH index ~2–4% of corpus size = ~150 GB | Banded LSH; single pass |
| Semantic | 3 CPU-hr per 100 GB → **153 CPU-hr** | Embeddings: 1 float32 per 64 tokens → 9.67×10¹¹/64 × 4 B = **60 GB** | Needs the embedding model resident |
| Vendored removal | ~40 CPU-hr per 100 GB → **204 CPU-hr** | ~0 | Per-repository file-set hashing |
| **Total** | **765 CPU-hr** | ~210 GB | |

**[INFERRED]** At `compute-feasibility.md` §12's cloud CPU rate of $0.05–0.20/hr, 765 CPU-hr = **$38–$153**. At the local machine's 16 threads, 765 CPU-hr ≈ 48 wall-clock hours — a weekend. **Dedup is not a cost problem; it is a scheduling problem.** That is the useful conclusion, because it means dedup can run on the local box while GPU rental is being arranged.

**[ESTIMATE]** Memory for the exact-dedup digest set: 1.478×10¹² tokens ÷ 3.7 B/token ≈ 4.0×10¹² bytes ≈ 40M files at 100 KB each. A 32-byte SHA-256 digest × 40M = 1.28 GB — fits the local 13.85 GiB RAM, or a sorted on-disk set of ~2.6 GB. **The exact tier needs no cluster.**

### 4.6 Dedup and the corpus version

**[INFERRED]** Dedup thresholds are **part of the corpus version**, not a pipeline setting. If the fuzzy threshold moves from 0.8 to 0.85, the corpus is a different corpus with a different manifest hash — which is why §8.3 versions by manifest content hash rather than a config label.

## 5. Filtering and quality

### 5.1 The three filter layers

**[INFERRED]** Filters are layered so the cheapest and most legally decisive run first:

| Layer | Purpose | Method | Removes (est.) |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **L-A. Legal** | Ensure nothing unpermitted enters | Per-item licence classification + whitelist (§5.4) | ~18% code, ~3% NL |
| **L-B. Privacy/safety** | Remove personal data, secrets, harmful content | Secret-pattern matching + PII classifier | ~1.2% NL, ~0.4% code |
| **L-C. Quality** | Remove low-value content | Heuristics + classifier (§5.2) | ~40–70% NL, ~8% code |

**[INFERRED]** Order matters: L-A must precede L-C, because running a quality classifier over content we were never allowed to keep is wasteful *and* is additional processing of material we should not have ingested.

### 5.2 Classifier vs heuristic

**[INFERRED]** This is the question the FineWeb-Edu result answers most directly.

| | Heuristic | Classifier |
| :- | :- | :- |
| Example | "≥ 3 code fences", "length in [200, 100000] chars", "no boilerplate phrase" | "is this document educational/explanatory?" |
| Cost | ~0.1 CPU-hr / 100 GB | ~8–15 CPU-hr / 100 GB (model inference) |
| Reproducibility | Perfect — deterministic rules | Needs a pinned model + weights hash |
| Explainability | Every decision traceable to a rule | Needs a score threshold and a margin |
| Recall on "bad" web text | Low — junk passes | High |
| Risk | Keeps noise, expensive downstream | Can remove good data; a judgement call |

**[KNOWN FACT]** FineWeb-Edu reports classifier-based educational-quality filtering from FineWeb, via Magpie. **[KNOWN FACT]** The Magicoder series reports the same idea applied to code. **[INFERRED]** Both argue: **a classifier cheap relative to the tokens it saves is worth running, because a token saved early is never downloaded, stored or deduped.**

**[INFERRED]** Our plan: **heuristics for anything with a hard, checkable criterion (licence, secrets, format); classifiers for anything requiring judgement (educational quality, code quality, PII likelihood).** No classifier runs without a pinned weights hash in the manifest, so the corpus is reproducible.

### 5.3 The "we removed X% and this is what we lost" note

**[INFERRED]** The most useful sentence in a data-quality report names the loss. Filtering is a trade, and an unstated trade is a decision made by default.

| Filter | Removed | What was lost, concretely |
| :- | :- | :- |
| Legal (L-A) | ~18% of code | **All GPL/AGPL/LGPL repositories**, in bulk. The classifier's false-negative rate scales directly with this number. A code model with no GPL in its training data has never seen a copyleft codebase as a *legitimate* artefact. A real capability gap, and the price of the licence chain. **Mitigation, honestly scoped:** a labelled corpus of permissively-licensed code **deliberately GPL-adjacent in style** (MIT projects reimplementing GPL approaches) covers a fraction. **What would settle the gap's size:** train a probe on GPL code and measure the delta — a stage-N+1 experiment. |
| Legal (L-A) | ~3% of NL | Reddit, and web text whose ToS forbids redistribution. The lost signal is informal, conversational, non-technical English — the register in which users phrase software problems. **Mitigation:** StackExchange (G1) and our own seeds cover a fraction. |
| Privacy (L-B) | ~1.2% of NL | Personal data, and with it any personal-data-bearing content. Also, correctly, the deepest form of human writing about individual problems. |
| Quality (L-C) | ~40–70% of NL | Web boilerplate, SEO-farm content, navigation chrome, ads, scraped Q&A fragments. The loss is **long-tail, low-traffic, high-quality text** — the wiki-quality material a 1B model would benefit from most, and that a classifier removes, because "educational" correlates with "popular". **The acknowledged bias of the FineWeb-Edu approach, and why §6.2 keeps a deliberate 5% "unfiltered web" slice.** |
| Quality (L-C) | ~8% of code | Truncated files, minified assets, generated code, test fixtures without tests. Loss: some legitimate short scripts, and — deliberately — **minified/bundled JS, of which we keep a 0.5% slice** as a format-robustness sample. |
| Dedup (§4) | 34.6% of raw tokens | Mostly the redundant tail, which is the point. Loss: **over-represented popular repositories** — the easiest to learn. Accepted deliberately. |

**[INFERRED]** The 5% unfiltered-web and 0.5% minified slices are not decoration. They are **deliberate, small, measured escapes from the filters**, retained because a corpus with zero filter bias is never inspected. They are also where contamination is most likely to hide — so they get the strictest filter ordering and are quarantined from evaluation partitions.

### 5.4 The licence filter (layer L-A), in three passes

**[INFERRED]** A licence classifier is only trustworthy in stages, and the stages have different error costs.

| Pass | Method | Purpose | Error cost |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **1. Manifest licence** | Where the corpus supplies a per-item licence field (CommitPackFT's `license`, OpenCodeInstruct's labels), use it | Zero-cost, authoritative where present | None if the field is correct |
| **2. File-level detection** | SPDX header match, then a classifier over the file's first 4 KB, then a repo-level prior from the API/manifest | The per-item licence for aggregate corpora | **False negatives are the dangerous ones** — a GPL file classified as MIT |
| **3. Repository propagation** | If a repo's licence is known, propagate to its files | Recovers files with no header | Over-propagation can mislabel a vendored directory |

**[INFERRED]** Pass 2 is the weak point, and the reason A9 (CodeSearchNet) is retained mainly as a **licence-detection training set**: its per-item labels are the point, and we can measure a classifier against them.

**[OBSERVED]** CommitPackFT's card states each sample's licence is in a `license` field, and that the corpus was built from permissively-licensed repositories. **[INFERRED]** Our A7 filter excludes per-sample licences `unknown`, `agpl-3.0`, `lgpl-2.1` (and, as policy, `gpl-3.0` and other copyleft), and records the excluded-licence distribution as a manifest statistic. **Pass 1, hard exclusion list, no classifier — the cheapest high-confidence path here.**

**[UNKNOWN]** The measured false-negative rate of our licence classifier on real code. **What would settle it:** a labelled set of 10,000 repositories with known licences, and precision/recall per class. Until then the classifier's output is marked **[UNKNOWN]** in every statistic that depends on it, and no corpus proportion figure is published without that caveat.

### 5.5 PII, secrets and unsafe content

**[INFERRED]** Three separate mechanisms, because they have different failure modes:

| Target | Mechanism | False-positive policy |
| :- | :- | :- |
| **Secrets** (keys, tokens, connection strings) | Deterministic regex + entropy checks on high-entropy strings, across all corpora | Low tolerance for false positives — a leaked key is unrecoverable |
| **PII** (names, emails, phones, addresses, IDs) | NER + regex on NL corpora; a lower threshold on code (emails/handles in comments) | Medium — a false positive costs one document |
| **Unsafe content** | A classifier over a small maintained taxonomy; applied to NL and code comments | High — recall preferred |

**[INFERRED]** Secrets scanning is the one filter with **no acceptable false-positive rate**: one missed private key reaching a public release is a security incident, while one false positive costs one document. We scan with a deliberately aggressive threshold and accept the loss.

**[OBSERVED]** §2's L-B filters are pipeline stages; the taxonomy and thresholds are recorded in the manifest, so a corpus revision is reproducible against the filter that produced it.

### 5.6 The measurement gate (M1)

**[INFERRED]** No corpus revision is promoted past a sampled-first gate. Before any full-scale ingest:

1. **Sample.** 10% stratified sample per corpus (by language, repository, licence class) - enough to characterise, small enough to cost ~$150 (§10).
2. **Measure.** Run the full filter chain; report per-corpus token count, dedup retention, licence-class distribution, PII/secret and quality removal rates, and the §4.4 token-budget effect.
3. **Decide.** Full ingest proceeds only if the measured retention, licence distribution and removal rates are within the expected envelope.
4. **Publish the sample statistics** with the corpus revision, so readers see the measured, not the assumed.

**[INFERRED]** This gate makes the cost model in §10 honest: it converts "our estimate of dedup rate" from assumption into measurement for ~$150, before the $100k-scale decisions are made.

### 6.3 The synthesis gap, and how it is closed

**[OBSERVED]** Publishable, permissively-licensed code-instruction data is on the order of 10⁶ rows (C3 111K, C18 437K, C2 66K, C17 51K, C13 11K, C12 ~1M incl. `code_`). **[OBSERVED]** `compute-feasibility.md`'s R2 rung is 1.5B parameters, Chinchilla-optimal for ~30B tokens.

**[INFERRED]** At 30B tokens, 1M instruction rows at ~400 tokens each = 4×10⁸ tokens, i.e. **1.3% of the token budget.** SFT data is therefore *never* the token bottleneck at pretraining scale — **the bottleneck is what the model learns before SFT; SFT sets format and behaviour, not facts.** The corollary: **we do not need a teacher model to build a viable SFT set.**

**[INFERRED]** The gap that *is* real is in **C-AGENT**: 1M synthetic agentic rows, mostly non-code, ~11K tool-call rows, ~65K repository-scoped SWE-Gym/SWE-smith instances. Enough to teach tool-call *format* and the shape of repository work, not long-horizon behaviour.

**[INFERRED]** How we close it, without a teacher and without the reference product:

1. **Our own open-weights generator.** An Apache-2.0 open-weights model (`Qwen3-0.6B`, `SmolLM2`) generates. **[INFERRED]** The generator's licence governs the generator; our tool schema, prompts and harness are our own original work; the *output* is ours. Not a §D.4 problem: the generator has no Anthropic path, and the constraint bans distillation *from the Services*.

**[INFERRED]** The rule that makes this defensible: **the provider adapter is not model intelligence.** §1.2's exclusion table bars the *reference product* as a generator. Using a publicly downloadable open-weights model, with our own prompts, schema and verification, is ordinary dataset construction — and the resulting corpus carries no Anthropic path.

### 6.4 The five-stage mixture and sequencing

**[INFERRED]** The corpus is consumed in stages, and the mixture changes as the model grows. `llm-development-roadmap.md` is the authority on the stage sequence (Stages D/E/F/G/H); we state the data side.

| Stage | What it is | Token budget (R1→R2) | Mixture | Dominant corpora |
| :- | :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **S1. Pretraining** | Broad capability from scratch | 6.0×10⁹ (R1) → 2.4×10¹⁰ (R2) | C-CODE 45%, C-NL 40%, C-DOC 10%, C-AGENT 5% | A1/A2, A5, B3, B5, F3 |

- **S2 is where the model becomes a code model.** Annealing on a documentation-and-code-heavy mixture is the highest-leverage single decision in the plan, and F3 (OpenCoder's `opc-annealing-corpus`) provides it permissively licensed. This is why the register includes it.

### 6.5 Mixture rules

**[INFERRED]** Four standing rules, so a corpus revision cannot quietly drift:

- **M-A. Every corpus has a class, a share, and a licence.** A corpus enters a mixture only with all three recorded. An unnamed share is a rounding error that becomes a policy.
- **M-B. Shards are licence-homogeneous.** §2.5's reasoning: a shard's licence is a function of its contents, so shards never mix content with incompatible obligations (CC-BY-SA with MIT, or ODC-By with a non-SA corpus that would thereby become SA-derived).
- **M-C. Any class change is a corpus revision.** Changing a share by more than 2 percentage points is a new revision with a new manifest hash.
- **M-D. The excluded slice is preserved.** The 5% unfiltered-web and 0.5% minified slices (§5.3) are held out of all evaluation partitions and never removed for being inconvenient.

### 6.6 How the mixture is measured

**[INFERRED]** The mixture is verified by measurement, not by inspection:

| Measurement | What it shows | How | Stage |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **MIX-1. Realised composition** | The token counts actually fed, per class, per corpus | The run log records the manifest shard and its token count | Every stage |
| **MIX-2. Perplexity by class** | Whether the model learned each class | Perplexity on held-out slices of each of the five classes | S1, S2, S3 ends |
| **MIX-3. Class regression** | Whether S3 degraded S1/S2 capability | Perplexity on S1/S2 held-out slices after S3 and S5 | After S3, S5 |
| **MIX-4. Filter-bias check** | Whether the filtered-out content would have mattered | Compare a model's perplexity on the unfiltered-web slice vs the filtered corpus | S2 |

**[INFERRED]** MIX-4 is the honest test of §5.3's concern about classifier bias. If the model handles the unfiltered slice no worse than the filtered corpus, the filter removed noise; if markedly better, we removed signal and the 5% escape slice is too small. **A falsifiable commitment, made before the measurement exists.**

## 7. Tokenization and packing

### 7.1 Vocabulary constraints

**[INFERRED]** The vocabulary is chosen against constraints we already know:

| Constraint | Source | Implication |
| :- | :- | :- |
| Model sizes R1 (360M) to R2 (1.5B) | `compute-feasibility.md` | Small models do not benefit from huge vocabs; 32K is ample for 360M and adequate for 1.5B |
| Storage 2 B/tok (uint16) vs 4 B/tok (uint32) | `compute-feasibility.md` §5 | Vocab ≤ 65,536 → uint16, halving corpus and checkpoint storage |
| Code is byte-heavy: punctuation, indentation, identifiers | — | A **byte-level BPE** is required; the whole vocabulary must be constructible from bytes |
| Fill-in-the-middle for code completion | — | Special tokens for FIM, plus handling in the training objective |

**[INFERRED]** **Chosen: a 32,000-token vocabulary, byte-level BPE, with 3 reserved FIM/control tokens and the rest of the 65,536 uint16 range unused (so a later move to 65,536 is a version bump, not a re-architecture).** This is a **[SPECULATIVE]** choice: §7.2's bytes-per-token measurement would confirm or refute it.

**[OBSERVED]** `compute-feasibility.md` §5's storage figures assume uint16 storage for vocabularies at or below 65,536, which fixes the ceiling.

### 7.2 Bytes to tokens

**[INFERRED]** The efficiency number drives everything downstream, so it is computed and then measured.

**Estimate.** For a mixed code+English corpus at 3.7 bytes/token: 1 token ≈ 3.7 bytes. Source volume for 7.2×10⁹ tokens = 2.66×10¹⁰ bytes ≈ 26.6 GB for R1; R2's 30B tokens = 1.11×10¹¹ B ≈ 111 GB. For the 1.478×10¹²-token raw corpus in §4.4: 5.47×10¹² B ≈ 5.5 TB — consistent with §4.5's 5.1 TB estimate (3.7 vs 3.6 bytes/token rounding).

**Measurement (M2).** Sample 10,000 documents (5,000 code, 5,000 text), tokenize with the candidate tokenizer, report the byte distribution. **What would settle the estimate:** the measured mean bytes/token on the actual mixture. Until then every storage and download figure here inherits the 3.7 assumption.

**[INFERRED]** Against vocabulary size: a 64K vocab might reach ~4.1 bytes/token, saving ~9% of storage, at the cost of a larger embedding matrix. **[ESTIMATE]** Embedding cost at R2: 65,536 × 1,536 (R2 hidden) × 2 B (uint16) = 201 MB per copy; three copies = 604 MB against a 1.5B-parameter model. A 32K vocab halves that to 302 MB. **At R1–R2 scale the vocabulary term matters and the storage saving does not.** Hence 32K.

### 7.3 Packing

**[INFERRED]** Packing is where most pipelines lose 10–30% of their compute. The rules:

1. **Pack to a fixed block size** (e.g. 4096 tokens) with no padding between documents.
2. **Mark document boundaries with a dedicated `<|endofdoc|>` token** so the model can learn boundaries, and a document's tokens are never split across a block in a way that confuses the objective.
3. **Never split a document mid-sequence without the boundary token** - a document is either whole or explicitly terminated.
4. **Length-bucketed shuffle** before packing: sort by length into buckets, shuffle within buckets with a fixed seed, then pack consecutive documents. This minimises the padding that unstratified packing would waste.
5. **Short documents are concatenated with the boundary token**, not padded.

**[ESTIMATE]** Packing efficiency: with length-bucketed shuffle, padding waste is ~1–3%; with random shuffle of the same corpus, ~8–15%. On R1's 7.2×10⁹ tokens, the gap between 3% and 12% waste is 0.29×10⁹ tokens of compute — small but real at `compute-feasibility.md` §12's A100 rate.

**[INFERRED]** **Measured by M3:** the fraction of tokens in the final stream that are padding, plus the fraction of documents that were split. Both go in the manifest.

### 7.4 Reproducibility

**[INFERRED]** The corpus revision is identified by the **hash of its manifest**, and the manifest records everything needed to rebuild the byte-identical stream:

| Manifest field | Value | Why |
| :- | :- | :- |
| `corpus_revision` | `tdata-<yyyymmdd>-<8 hex>` | Human-readable handle |
| `manifest_sha256` | SHA-256 over the canonical manifest | **The run's data identity** |
| Per-shard entries | corpus id, source revision, licence class, token count, SHA-256 of the shard file | Byte-level identity of every input |
| `tokenizer_sha256` | Hash of the tokenizer artefact | The tokenization is part of the identity |
| `pack_seed` | Integer | The shuffle is part of the identity |
| `filter_config_sha256` | Hash of the filter configuration | The filters are part of the identity |
| `dedup_config` | Thresholds, MinHash permutation count and seed | The dedup is part of the identity |
| `source_dates` | Per-corpus fetch timestamp | Reproducibility depends on knowing *when* the bytes were fetched, because upstream changes |
| `code_commit` | Git commit of the pipeline that produced it | The processing code is part of the identity |

**[INFERRED]** The three "part of the identity" classes are the ones people forget. A corpus is not "the training data"; it is "the training data as produced by *this* tokenizer, *these* thresholds, *this* seed, by *this* code, from *these* upstream bytes as of *that* date". A run record naming only the dataset is not reproducible, nor auditable.

**[KNOWN FACT]** The Stack v2's Terms of Use point 4 (§2.4) requires the user to update to the most recent usable version. **[INFERRED]** That makes `source_dates` a compliance field, not a convenience: it is how we demonstrate which upstream version we ingested, and therefore which removal requests our revision predates.

## 8. Pipeline architecture

### 8.1 The twelve stages

**[INFERRED]** Each stage consumes an artefact and produces an artefact. No stage reads and writes in place; the artefact boundary is what makes the corpus reproducible and the cost attributable.

| # | Stage | Input | Output | Key property |
| :- | :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **0** | **Fetch** | Corpus id + source revision | Raw shards (parquet/JSONL) | Each shard gets a provenance record (DR1) |
| **1** | **Verify licence** | Raw shards | Licence-classified shards | Runs *before* any content processing |
| **2** | **Normalise** | Raw shards | Normalised text | The rule is hashed into the manifest |
| **3** | **Exact dedup** | Normalised | Deduped | SHA-256 set |
| **4** | **Fuzzy dedup** | Deduped | Near-deduped | MinHash/LSH |
| **5** | **Vendored removal** | Near-deduped | Repo-clean | Directory-level |
| **6** | **Semantic dedup** | Repo-clean | Semantic-clean | Optional, §4.2 tier 3 |
| **7** | **Filter** | Deduped | Filtered | L-A legal → L-B privacy → L-C quality, in that order |
| **8** | **Contamination filter** | Filtered | C-filtered | §3.5's five filters; the suite fingerprint is the input |
| **9** | **Tokenize + pack** | C-filtered | Token stream | §7 |
| **10** | **Shard + mix** | Token stream | Mixture shards | Licence-homogeneous (§6.5 M-B) |
| **11** | **Manifest + receipt** | Shards | `manifest.json` + build receipt | §8.2–8.4 |

**[INFERRED]** Stage 1 precedes stage 2 deliberately: **we must not normalise, deduplicate or embed content we are not permitted to hold.** Stage 8 is separate from stage 7 because contamination filtering has a different failure cost and a different reviewer.

### 8.2 The build receipt

**[INFERRED]** Every corpus revision emits a receipt, human-readable and machine-checkable:

- corpus revision id and `manifest_sha256`
- per-corpus: source URL, upstream revision, fetch date, licence (packaging and per-item class), shard count, token count, bytes, and the SHA-256 of each shard
- the filter, dedup and tokenizer configuration hashes
- the suite fingerprint used by the contamination filter
- the filter statistics from §5 (what was removed and why)
- the number of items removed by an upstream removal request since the previous revision (§8.3)
- the sign-off record from §9

**[INFERRED]** The receipt is what makes the corpus reviewable by someone who does not trust us. A corpus without one is an artefact of unknown composition; with one, it has a stated composition, method and licence chain.

### 8.3 Corpus revision versioning

**[INFERRED]** The corpus is a **DAG of revisions**, not a sequence of snapshots.

- A revision names its **parent** revision and the **stage** from which it was rebuilt.
- **We never patch a revision.** A change in a filter, threshold, token count or source is a new revision rebuilt from the named stage. Patching would leave a corpus whose parts have different provenance dates — exactly the state in which the Stack v2 removal-request obligation becomes impossible to satisfy.

**[INFERRED]** Rebuild-from-stage-N, not rebuild-from-zero, is what makes this affordable: a dedup threshold change rebuilds from stage 3; a mixture change from stage 10; a source removal from stage 0.

### 8.4 Recording the data version in the run

**[INFERRED]** Every training run records in its run manifest:

| Field | Value |
| :- | :- |
| `corpus_revision` | e.g. `tdata-20261021-a3f19c22` |
| `manifest_sha256` | The run's data identity |
| `tokenizer_sha256` | Tokenizer identity |
| `pack_seed` | Packing seed |
| `code_commit` | Pipeline commit |
| `source_dates` | Per-corpus fetch timestamps |
| `mixture_class_shares` | Realised per-class token shares (MIX-1) |
| `suite_fingerprint` | Contamination fingerprint used |

**[INFERRED]** The rule: **a run is identified by (code commit, manifest hash, tokenizer hash, seed, data version).** Two runs differing in any of these are different runs, and their results are not comparable without saying so — the discipline `benchmark-and-evaluation-plan.md` applies to evaluation runs, and what lets a later reader reconstruct what was trained.

### 8.5 Storage layout

**[INFERRED]** A simple, inspectable layout:

```
corpus/
  <corpus_revision>/
    manifest.json
    receipt.json
    shards/
      <class>-<licence_class>-<shard_id>.bin   # uint16 token stream
    provenance/
      per_item.parquet                          # per-item licence, source, SWHID
      swkids.txt                                # if Stack-derived
    logs/
      stage-<n>.log
      filter_stats.json
```

**[INFERRED]** **Raw shards are not retained.** The raw download is deleted after stage 7, keeping only the filtered, tokenized artefacts plus provenance. **[ESTIMATE]** The saving is 5.5 TB → ~1.6 TB retained for a 1.478×10¹²-token raw corpus; at `compute-feasibility.md` §12's $0.02-$0.09/GB/month that is **$118-$533/month**. The cost is that a filter change forces a re-download, priced at ~$400 in §10.6. **The sampled-first policy (§10.2) resolves it: we iterate on 10% samples; only the final revision pays the full re-fetch.**

## 9. Rights ledger and audit trail

### 9.1 The gate, restated as a ledger precondition

**[OBSERVED]** `licensing-and-provenance-review.md` §9.1's gate. **[INFERRED]** This document's contribution is the *form* it takes: a per-corpus decision record that must exist **before** a run, not after.

**[INFERRED]** The rule that matters operationally: **the rights ledger entry is a precondition for ingestion, not a report of what happened.** A corpus with no entry is not ingested; it is a proposal.

### 9.2 Entry schema

**[INFERRED]** One entry per corpus, per revision, in a machine-readable ledger.

| Field | Content |
| :- | :- |
| `corpus_id` | Hub id or URL |
| `source_revision` | Upstream commit/version/snapshot date |
| `fetched_at` | UTC timestamp |
| `licence_packaging` | SPDX id, or `other` + text ref |
| `licence_per_item` | `single` or `inherited` (per-item; item licences in `provenance/`) |
| `obligation_set` | Which apply: attribution, share-alike, non-commercial, field-of-use, patent-grant, notice-retention, update |
| `share_alike` | Boolean — drives §6.5 M-B's shard homogeneity |
| `commercial_use` | Permitted / conditional / prohibited |
| `d4_status` | `safe` / `excluded` + the reason |
| `contamination_status` | Filter run id, suite fingerprint, removal counts by C-level |
| `provenance_method` | How per-item provenance is established (SWHID / repo-level / manifest / none) |
| `obligations_discharged` | How each obligation is discharged in the shipped artefact |
| `open_questions` | OQ ids |
| `signoff` | Name, date, and the ledger version |

**[INFERRED]** `obligations_discharged` is the field that distinguishes a ledger from a filing. A licence review that identifies an obligation without recording how it is met has not discharged it.

### 9.3 The shard rule

**[INFERRED]** **The shard is the unit of licensing.** A shard is a homogeneous, self-contained token stream whose contents carry the same obligation set. Shards never mix obligation sets that differ in kind — a share-alike shard is never merged with a non-share-alike one, and a non-commercial shard never merges with anything (there are none; §2 excludes them).

**[INFERRED]** Obligations are *contagious*: if a CC-BY-SA shard is mixed with MIT content and the result redistributed, "is the MIT content now SA-licensed?" has a defensible answer of "yes, in the combined work". **Keeping shards separate makes the answer irrelevant**, at the cost of some duplication in the stream — a cheap price for a clean licence chain.

### 9.4 The per-item provenance record

**[INFERRED]** For regime-2 corpora, each item carries: source URL, repository identifier, SWHID, per-item licence, and the attribution string. **[KNOWN FACT]** The Stack v2's ToU point 3 states provenance is provided per data point and original licences must be followed including attribution clauses; the SWH statement requires publishing the SWHIDs.

**[INFERRED]** So the provenance record is not internal bookkeeping — it is a **publishable artefact**, and §2.4's SWH-principles condition is discharged by shipping it with the model release. A revision without a `provenance/` directory is not releasable under the Stack's terms.

### 9.5 Three audit levels

**[INFERRED]** The audit is proportional, and all three run before a release.

| Level | Question | Method | Cost |
| :- | :- | :- | :- |
| **L1. Ledger self-check** | Does every corpus in the manifest have a complete entry, and does every obligation have a discharge? | Automated schema validation | ~0 |
| **L2. Sample audit** | Do the per-item records match reality? | 200 items per corpus: re-fetch the source, confirm licence and attribution string | ~4 CPU-hr + egress |
| **L3. Red-team licence** | Is there a corpus in the release whose licence we got wrong? | Independent review of the 5 largest shards + every `other` or `inherited` corpus | ~2 person-days |

**[INFERRED]** L3's scope is the point: **the review effort goes where the uncertainty is** — corpora with a `other` packaging licence or per-item inheritance, not the MIT corpora. This is why §2 has two licence columns.

### 9.6 What the ledger does not do

**[INFERRED]** The ledger is not legal advice, and it does not resolve §2.5's open questions (ODC-By's extent over weights, C-UDA's field-of-use text). It records those as OQ ids and stops. **Its job is to make uncertainty visible and attributable before a run, not to make it disappear.**

## 10. Cost

### 10.1 Basis and rates

**[OBSERVED]** From `compute-feasibility.md` §12, observed 2026-10-21: cloud CPU **$0.05–$0.20/hr**; A100 **$2.79/hr**; object storage **$0.02–$0.09/GB/month**; a data pipeline **200–600 CPU-hr = $30–$250**; tokenizer+tooling **$50/$150/$400** (small/medium/large).

**[OBSERVED]** Local hardware: AMD Ryzen 7 5700U, 8c/16t, 13.85 GiB RAM, 159.2 GB free disk, no CUDA, torch 2.14.1+cpu, 1.72×10¹¹ FP32 FLOP/s measured. **[INFERRED]** So data-pipeline work that fits in 13.85 GiB RAM and 159.2 GB of disk runs locally at zero marginal cost; only work exceeding that must be rented. Same conclusion as `compute-feasibility.md` §13.4: *do the data pipeline on free CPU; do the training on rented GPUs.*

### 10.2 The sampled-first policy

**[INFERRED]** This is the plan's most important cost decision, following from two arithmetic results in this section.

**Result 1 — full-corpus storage.** **[ESTIMATE]** 1.478×10¹² raw tokens × 3.7 B/token = 5.47×10¹² B = **5.47 TB**. At $0.02–$0.09/GB/month for 3 months: 5,470 GB × 3 × $0.02–$0.09 = **$328–$1,477/month**, i.e. **$984–$4,431** for three months; a year is $3,284–$14,778. **[INFERRED]** Not prohibitive, but not a rounding error.

**Result 2 — full-corpus processing waste.** **[ESTIMATE]** Download + normalise + dedup + filter + embed for 1.478×10¹² tokens costs 765 CPU-hr of dedup alone (§4.5) plus ~200–400 for the rest → **1,000–1,200 CPU-hr** = $50–$240, plus egress. **[INFERRED]** But the usable budget is 7.2×10⁹ tokens (R1) — **0.49% of the raw corpus.** Processing the other 99.51% is a 70:1 waste of compute and storage, spent on a revision that will be re-tuned.

**Therefore:** **[INFERRED] process a 10% stratified sample first, measure, then commit.** Sample cost: 10% of the download, 10% of the processing (76–120 CPU-hr), 10% of the storage (547 GB, $33–$148/month). **[ESTIMATE]** Total → **$150–$400** including egress, versus $1,000+ for one full pass.
